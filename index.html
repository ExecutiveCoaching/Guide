CIPD Guide Summary: Procuring Executive Coaching Services

Executive coaching (EC) has evolved from a ‘sounding board’ into a strategic intervention. Research suggests that EC contributes to delivering personal, professional, and organisational goals. However, effective planning must navigate the ‘perception gap’ between a leader’s enthusiasm and their actual objective performance.

This guide summary outlines five critical pillars for effective procurement and application:

1. Defining the Scope

  • EC is a formal, three-way partnership between the coach, the executive, and the organisation, clearly distinguished from mentoring, remedial support, and general workplace coaching.
  • Within a structured agreement, the coach applies various methodologies to achieve mutually defined goals focused on three core pillars: professional performance, personal well-being, and organisational effectiveness.
  • To ensure the efficacy of this partnership, it is essential to explicitly define the goals, expectations, and the specific coaching approach from the outset.

2. Buying and Selection

  • Prioritise coaches with verified professional qualifications, relevant experience, and a proven track record of applying structured, psychologically informed frameworks.
  • These frameworks are essential for ensuring skills transfer successfully back to the workplace.

3. Readiness and Investment Risks

  • Leaders most in need of development may resist the process. Beyond simple willingness, organisations should assess an executive’s readiness to change.
  • The ability to manage emotions and reactions is a critical foundation; pre-coaching assessments help avoid investing in leaders who are resistant to self-reflection.

4. Evaluating Success

  • Address the ‘perception gap’ where executives may report higher personal gains than their peers observe.
  • Relying solely on executive feedback forms can lead to biased results.
  • Addressing this requires objective and valid measurements of ROI (Return on Investment) to assess actual impact.
  • Progress should be triangulated using feedback from others to provide objective ratings.

5. Managing for Lasting Impact

  • To ensure change is permanent, maintain a constructive alignment involving the coach, executive, HR, and the CEO.
  • Programmes must include a dedicated phase for embedding new habits.
  • Growth requires moving from intensive intervention to a maintenance phase where new leadership behaviours are practised consistently and become routine.
